# The Index Went Up. Most Stocks Went Down.

*Weekly Market Newsletter · August 28, 2026*

The S&P added 0.5% on the week and finished at 7711.76, about 1.1% under the record it set on August 13. Sounds fine. Then you look under the hood and find 1,214 stocks fell while only 708 rose. A green tape carried by a handful of giants while the average stock quietly slides — that's the tension this week, and the issue is about where the money went while the index looked calm. Our read of ~1,900 US names by relative strength says it left drug makers, sporting-goods retail and computer hardware, and turned up in financials, parts of health care, precious metals and, at the speculative edge, crypto.

## 1. The lead story: a thin rally

MSFT rose 6.3% and climbed to #66 on our board, up 70 spots. META rose 5.1%. AAPL added 3.3%. Those moves, plus a few more, are most of your index gain. That's the mega-cap engine doing its usual trick: a small number of trillion-dollar names lift the cap-weighted average while the median stock does nothing good.

The breadth tells the truer story. Only 49.85% of stocks sit above their 50-day line, a coin flip. Advancers lost to decliners by a wide margin. So the index printing near a record is real, but it's narrow, and narrow rallies live or die on whether leadership broadens out or the generals roll over. Right now the generals are carrying, and the troops are split by sector.

## 3. The Fed talks tough, the jobs number wobbles

Jackson Hole was the macro event, and the message from Fed chair Warsh was not what a rate-cut crowd wanted: "we have work to do on inflation," with the Fed's preferred PCE gauge described as sticky and rate-hike expectations edging up rather than down. CPI is still running 3.4% year over year. The Fed-funds target upper bound sits at 3.63%.

A hawkish Fed is a headwind for the most expensive, longest-duration corners of the market, part of why chips and hardware keep lagging even as software earnings land well. When the discount rate might go up instead of down, the market pays less for far-off growth.

The jobs picture muddies it. The last nonfarm payrolls print was *negative 23,000*, an outright drop, yet weekly jobless claims slipped to around 203,000, which is low, and unemployment holds at 4.1%. The labor market is cooling at the hiring margin without cracking on layoffs. The 10-year yield barely moved (4.67%, down 2bp), the 2-year ticked up to 4.2%, and the 2s10s curve stayed positive at 47bp. No stress in rates. Next Friday's jobs report is the release that settles which of those two signals the Fed leans on.

## 4. Oil falls, and the rotation shows its hand

Brent crude dropped 6.7% on the week to 88.10. No Middle-East shock drove it. The headlines are about soft demand and stabilizing supply, the kind of decline that helps the consumer and pressures energy producers. Cheaper fuel is a quiet tailwind under retail and travel, and a headwind for the drillers.

On the board, the rotation was sharp and specific. Drug Manufacturers – Specialty & Generic fell from #28 to #77. Leisure Facilities dropped from #52 to #99. Computer Hardware slid #64 to #108. Moving the other way: Education Services surged #89 to #42, and Fertilizers & Agricultural Chemicals jumped #68 to #25.

The retail earnings this week split the same way the tape did. Abercrombie & Fitch jumped 36% after reporting, and Gap rose 19%. But Dick's Sporting Goods fell 26% on its print, Burlington dropped 16%, and Best Buy slipped 4% even after raising full-year guidance and naming a new CEO. That's not a sector you buy as a block. It's a stock-picker's aisle right now, winners and losers within the same week.

Software, by contrast, mostly delivered. Salesforce rose 22% on earnings, CrowdStrike 14%, Elastic 16%, Box 6%. Which brings up a wrinkle worth flagging.

## 5. Theme of the Week: how to own the crypto economy

Our rotating teaching spotlight this week is Crypto & Blockchain, a narrow, high-beta slice of the market that the platform maps as a value chain. This is a lesson in how the chain works, not a call to pile in and not a claim about the whole market.

Think of the crypto economy as a supply chain for digital money, same as oil has wellheads, pipelines and gas stations. At the raw end sits compute, the power-hungry data centers and bitcoin miners that secure the network (CORZ, IREN, APLD; the miners WULF, MARA, RIOT, CLSK, CIFR). Money flows to the chokepoint and away from the commodity end, and right now the commodity end is cold: our node-momentum score has compute at 27 out of 100 and miners at 33. IREN even reported this week and fell 15%. Being the picks-and-shovels layer doesn't help when the shovels are a commodity.

Move down the chain to the exchanges and brokers, the toll booths where people actually trade (COIN, HOOD, GLXY), and momentum jumps to 80. Coinbase climbed 429 rank spots this week to #361 even though its price slipped. Toll booths get paid on volume, and volume is back.

The hottest links are at the money end. Treasuries, companies that hold crypto on their balance sheet, led by Strategy (MSTR), score 94, and MSTR rocketed 527 spots to #184. And the single strongest node is **stablecoins**, at 96, where Circle (CRCL) sits at RS #121 of ~1,900. Stablecoins are the plumbing that turns blockchain into an actual payments rail, and that's where the concentration is: money flows to the chokepoint, which right now is the thing that moves dollars around, not the thing that mines them.

The screen also flags APLD as cheapest on growth-adjusted sales, price-to-sales of 11.8 against 407% year-over-year revenue growth. That is not a recommendation; it's a reminder that "cheap" and "safe" are different words. This whole theme is early, thin and volatile. Treat it as an education in where value concentrates in a chain, then do your own homework before you touch any of it.

*Methodology. The Algo is a long-only, rules-based paper strategy on a $50,000 notional sleeve, trading the same ~1,900-stock universe. A name is bought only if it clears every gate: its sector ranks near the top of the board with broad participation, earnings are growing year-over-year in both revenue and profit, it isn't already held, and it's past a 21-day cooldown. Qualifying setups are sorted into four conviction tiers by how many independent signals fire — from T1 (multiple 'strong' chart patterns, risked at 1.5% of the sleeve) down to T4 (relative-strength acceleration, 0.5%). Size is set by risk, not dollars: each trade is sized so a stop 2×ATR below entry costs only that tier's risk budget, capped at $2,000–$6,000. Exits are mechanical — scale out a third at +2R and a third at +4R (1R = the initial stop distance), trail the remainder by 2×ATR, book some after a +25% spike, and time out positions left flat after 25 trading days. New entries pause if the book is down 2% on the day or 8% from its peak. Signals are generated after the close and filled the next session at the live price plus 0.15% slippage. These are educational paper results — not advice, and not a live account.*

## 11. Glossary

- **Our rank / the board:** we rank every US stock we track by relative strength, #1 strongest. "#66 in our system" = 66th-strongest of ~1,900.
- **Relative strength (RS):** performance versus the rest of the market. Rising RS = outperforming.
- **Moving averages (20/50/200-day):** the trend lines. Healthy uptrend = price above a rising 20-day, itself above the 200. "Extended" = far above the 20-day and prone to a pullback.
- **VIX:** the fear gauge. Below 20 = calm.
- **Credit spreads:** extra yield demanded on risky corporate bonds vs safe government bonds. Widening = early warning.
- **Breadth:** how many stocks participate. Half above the 50-day is a coin flip, not a broad rally.
- **High-yield / junk bonds (HYG):** riskier companies' bonds. Falling price = credit stress spreading; steady/up = calm.
- **Safe havens (gold, the dollar):** where money runs when scared. Flat gold through a scary week means the market read it as noise.
- **2s10s:** the 10-year Treasury yield minus the 2-year. Positive and steepening (47bp now) is the un-inverted, non-recession-warning shape.

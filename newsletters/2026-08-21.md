# Money Left the Banks and the Chips. It Didn't Leave the Market.

*Issue 34 · August 21, 2026 · a weekly read built on ranking ~1,900 US stocks by relative strength every day*

The S&P slipped 1.4% on the week and closed 1.6% below its August 13 high, and yet almost twice as many stocks rose as fell. That's the tension this week. The index looked tired while the market underneath it churned hard: out of steel, banks and chips, into health care, energy and a fistful of commodity names. Oil and gold both jumped, which tells you where the nervous money went, and where it didn't.

The S&P fell 1.4% to 7674.37, still comfortably above its rising 200-day (7129.67). Advancers beat decliners 1268 to 658. A red index over a green market is the signature of rotation, not distribution. Money isn't leaving. It's changing seats.

Our board shows the seat change in high definition. Steel fell from #3 to #80 as a peer group. Diversified Banks went from #18 to #93. Electronic Components dropped #29 to #97, and Electronic Equipment & Instruments #23 to #82. On the other side, Soft Drinks & Non-alcoholic Beverages vaulted from #110 to #43, and Health Care supplied 9 of the top 25 weekly climbers, with Financials adding 6 and Energy 4.

So the groups that led the spring, big banks and the electronics complex tied to the AI build-out, spent this week getting sold, while defensive-growth like biotech and medical devices and the inflation-sensitive corner in energy and materials took the baton. The index number hides all of that. The board doesn't.

## 3. Oil and gold both spiked — read them together, not apart

Brent crude rose 6.6% on the week, back above the $90s, on renewed US–Iran tension around the Strait of Hormuz. That's the headline behind every "Sensex falls on crude" wire and behind Japan printing its hottest inflation of the year on energy costs. When the world's chokepoint for seaborne oil gets a scare, the premium shows up in Brent first.

Gold did something similar, up 6.8%. Two safe-ish assets rising at once usually means fear, so look at the rest of the panel before you conclude fear owns the tape. The VIX is asleep at 15.13. High-yield bonds barely moved (-0.1%). The dollar fell 0.9% on the week, which is the opposite of a flight-to-safety bid. So the anxiety is concentrated in the commodity aisle, oil on geopolitics and gold on a mix of geopolitics and inflation worry, while credit and equity-vol shrugged.

That distinction matters. If gold, junk and the dollar had all turned together, I'd tell you the market was hedging for real. Instead you've got a supply-and-inflation story in commodities that hasn't infected the plumbing. The tell that it's inflation and not just war: the 10-year yield rose 6bp to 4.69% the same week gold rallied. Growth-fear buys gold and sells yields; inflation-fear buys gold and sells bonds. This looks like the second one.

The clean read-through is Energy on our watchlist, which I'll get to. The dirtier one is that a persistent $90s Brent keeps upward pressure on CPI, which already sits at 3.4%.

## 4. The Fed's strange spot, and a curve that's steepening

Most of the year the debate was when the Fed cuts. Now the betting markets are chewing on whether it *hikes* in September. Odds of a hike reportedly fell to around one-in-four, but the fact that a hike is even on the table tells you inflation hasn't been beaten. Fed funds sit at 3.63% (upper target), CPI at 3.4%, and the last payrolls print was actually *negative* at -23k. A central bank staring at soft jobs and sticky prices is the hardest kind to trade around.

The bond market's own vote: the 2s10s spread is a positive 50bp and steepening, with the 10-year at 4.69% and the 2-year at 4.19%. A steepening curve off a positive base usually says growth-and-inflation, not imminent recession, and it fits the oil-and-gold picture above. A rising long yield is a headwind for the most expensive, longest-duration stocks, which is one more reason the chip and software mega-caps couldn't get out of their own way this week.

Weekly jobless claims land next Thursday and matter more than usual. Claims *dropped* to 206k in the latest read, layoffs still sparse. The puzzle is that it's easy to keep a job and hard to find one. Another low claims number keeps the hawks talking.

## 5. Theme of the Week — Food & Agriculture, from the seed to the table

Set the market noise aside for a minute. This week's teaching spotlight is the food supply chain, a clean way to see how money picks a link in a chain and parks there.

Think of getting food to a plate as a relay with five runners. First, the inputs that make a crop possible: seeds and fertilizer. Then the machinery that plants and harvests it. Then animal health, keeping livestock alive and productive. Then the processors and ingredient makers who turn raw commodities into things you'd actually eat. Finally, the distributors who truck it to restaurants and stores. Value flows to whichever runner is scarcest at the moment, the link everyone else has to go through.

Right now our platform's momentum scores (a 0-100 blend of rank, trend and price vs the 50-day, where 50 is dead average) say the baton is with the front of the chain. Seeds and fertilizer is the hottest link at 56, the names there being CF, CTVA, FMC, MOS and NTR. Processing sits close behind at 54 (ADM, BG, DAR, INGR). Equipment is middling at 50 (AGCO, CNH, DE, LNN). Animal health lags at 38, and distribution at 43. The theme as a whole warmed up fast, from a median 36 a week ago to 49 now.

Why the front of the chain? When crop prices and input costs move, the fertilizer and seed makers feel it first. They sit at the pinch point where a whole season's economics gets set. The cost flows downstream to the processor and the distributor, but the pricing power tends to sit upstream. Money's been following that logic.

Two names the data flagged, and neither is advice, just where to start reading. US Foods (USFD) is the strongest single member at a momentum score of 80 and an RS rank of #319, a distributor bucking its own sleepy link. And Bunge (BG) screens as the cheapest on growth-adjusted sales, with a price-to-sales ratio of 0.2 against 88% year-over-year revenue growth, a number worth understanding before you trust it, because a figure that extreme usually means a merger or an accounting quirk is inflating the top line. Do the homework; that's the point of the spotlight.

One housekeeping note: this theme covers only part of the ~1,900-name universe. It's this week's lesson, not a call that agriculture is about to lead the market. The broad rotation read lives in the next section.

*Methodology. The Algo is a long-only, rules-based paper strategy on a $50,000 notional sleeve, trading the same ~1,900-stock universe. A name is bought only if it clears every gate: its sector ranks near the top of the board with broad participation, earnings are growing year-over-year in both revenue and profit, it isn't already held, and it's past a 21-day cooldown. Qualifying setups are sorted into four conviction tiers by how many independent signals fire — from T1 (multiple 'strong' chart patterns, risked at 1.5% of the sleeve) down to T4 (relative-strength acceleration, 0.5%). Size is set by risk, not dollars: each trade is sized so a stop 2×ATR below entry costs only that tier's risk budget, capped at $2,000–$6,000. Exits are mechanical — scale out a third at +2R and a third at +4R (1R = the initial stop distance), trail the remainder by 2×ATR, book some after a +25% spike, and time out positions left flat after 25 trading days. New entries pause if the book is down 2% on the day or 8% from its peak. Signals are generated after the close and filled the next session at the live price plus 0.15% slippage. These are educational paper results — not advice, and not a live account.*

## 11. Glossary

- **Our rank / the board:** we rank every US stock we track by relative strength, #1 strongest. "#8 in our system" = 8th-strongest of ~1,900.
- **Relative strength (RS):** performance vs the rest of the market. Rising RS = outperforming.
- **Moving averages (20/50/200-day):** the trend lines. A healthy uptrend has price above a rising 20-day, itself above the 200-day. "Extended" = far above the 20-day and prone to a pullback.
- **VIX:** the market's fear gauge. Below 20 = calm.
- **Credit spreads:** the extra yield on risky corporate bonds vs safe government bonds. Widening = warning; tight = calm.
- **Treasury yields / 2s10s:** the 10-year vs 2-year government borrowing rate; the spread is 10y minus 2y. A rising 10-year pressures expensive, long-duration stocks.
- **Breadth:** how many stocks participate. Strong breadth = broad, healthy rally.
- **High-yield / junk bonds:** bonds from riskier companies. Falling prices are an early credit-stress signal.
- **Safe havens (gold, the dollar):** where money runs when it's scared. A rally signals real fear; flat through a scary headline signals calm.
- **Node / momentum score:** the platform's 0-100 composite of RS rank, trend and price vs the 50-day. 50 is average, not a rank.

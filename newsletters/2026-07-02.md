# The Rally Caught Up to Its Own Guts

*Issue 32 · Thursday, July 2, 2026*

Last week the index looked sick while the average stock looked fine. This week the two closed the gap: the S&P 500 rose 1.8%, breadth stayed broad, and the money kept moving into the same quiet rooms it found a week ago. The catch is that the Fed conversation turned the other way. The talk now is "plan for higher rates," and gold just hit an annual low on it.

The view here rests on ranking about 1,900 US stocks every day by relative strength and watching where the strength settles. Right now it's settling into payments, hospitals, and software, and away from the metals and the builders.

## 2. The Fed talk turned hawkish, and gold noticed

Under the good tape, the rate story got less friendly. The loudest Fed chatter this week was about Kevin Warsh and a "plan for higher rates," the idea that the next Fed is more likely to keep policy tight than to ease into it. Gold hit an annual low on that bet, with sticky inflation cited as the weight. CPI is still running at 4.2% year over year, and the Fed-funds target sits at 3.63% at the upper bound.

Our rates data is as of June 25 and hasn't refreshed, so I'll flag that rather than pretend it's live: the 10-year was 4.4% (down 6bp), the 2-year 4.09% (down 10bp), and the 2s10s spread a positive, healthy 31bp. A normal upward-sloping curve, in other words, even as the hawks get louder.

The tension is the same one I've been pointing at, just sharper. A bond market with a steepening curve is telling you growth holds and policy stays roughly put. The Warsh crowd and a gold price at annual lows are telling you the risk is a hike. Both can't be right for long. If yields back up, the groups that lead on cheap money get hit first, and this week that would mean the software and payments names now doing the leading.

## 5. Theme of the week: copper, and why the chokepoint is empty

This week's teaching spotlight is Copper and Critical Minerals, the picks-and-shovels of everything electric. Our platform maps value-chain themes that cover only part of the 1,900-stock universe, so this is a lesson in how the chain works, not a coverage claim and not a rotation call.

Picture the chain in four links. At the bottom are the copper miners who dig the metal out of the ground (Freeport, Southern Copper, Ero, Teck). Above them sit the diversified giants who mine everything (BHP, Rio, Vale). Off to the side are the rare-earth and magnet suppliers whose materials modern motors and datacenters can't run without (MP, USA Rare Earth). And at the top sit the royalty financiers (Franco-Nevada, Royal Gold, Wheaton), who don't dig anything themselves. They hand a miner cash up front in exchange for a permanent cut of future production. Think of a royalty company as the toll booth on the mine: it takes a slice of every ounce that comes out, without ever running a shovel or paying a fuel bill.

In a normal cycle, when metal demand runs hot, money flows to whichever link is the bottleneck, and the royalty toll booths tend to hold up best because they carry the least operating risk. This week, there is no hot link. The whole theme is cooling, with median member momentum down to 10 out of 100 from 14 a week ago. The royalty link is the "hottest," but only in the sense of least cold, at 19 out of 100. Look at the standout: Royal Gold is the relative leader of the group and it ranks #1,783 of about 1,900 on our board. When your leader sits near the bottom of the whole market, the group isn't leading anything.

That lines up with the macro. Gold hit an annual low this week on rate-hike bets, and higher-for-longer rates raise the cost of holding metals that pay you nothing. The one name that screens interesting on fundamentals is Ero Copper, cheapest of the group on growth-adjusted sales at a price-to-sales of 3.0 against 110% revenue growth. That's a screen, not a recommendation, and a cheap stock in a weak chain is still swimming against the current. The lesson this week is the discipline itself: a theme can be structurally important to the future and still be a bad place to put money today. Respect the trend in front of you, and the trend in copper is down.

*Methodology. The Algo is a long-only, rules-based paper strategy on a $50,000 notional sleeve, trading the same ~1,900-stock universe. A name is bought only if it clears every gate: its sector ranks near the top of the board with broad participation, earnings are growing year-over-year in both revenue and profit, it isn't already held, and it's past a 21-day cooldown. Qualifying setups are sorted into four conviction tiers by how many independent signals fire — from T1 (multiple 'strong' chart patterns, risked at 1.5% of the sleeve) down to T4 (relative-strength acceleration, 0.5%). Size is set by risk, not dollars: each trade is sized so a stop 2×ATR below entry costs only that tier's risk budget, capped at $2,000–$6,000. Exits are mechanical — scale out a third at +2R and a third at +4R (1R = the initial stop distance), trail the remainder by 2×ATR, book some after a +25% spike, and time out positions left flat after 25 trading days. New entries pause if the book is down 2% on the day or 8% from its peak. Signals are generated after the close and filled the next session at the live price plus 0.15% slippage. These are educational paper results — not advice, and not a live account.*

## 11. Glossary

- **Our rank / the board:** we rank every US stock we track by relative strength, #1 strongest. "#6 in our system" means 6th-strongest of about 1,900.
- **Relative strength (RS):** how a stock performs against the rest of the market, not on its own. Rising RS means it's outpacing the pack.
- **Moving averages (20/50/200-day):** the average price over that many days, the trend lines. A healthy uptrend has price above a rising 20-day, which sits above the 200-day. "Stretched" means price has run far above the 20-day and is prone to snap back.
- **VIX:** the market's fear gauge. Below 20 is calm; above 20 is stress.
- **Credit spreads:** the extra yield investors demand to hold risky corporate bonds over safe government bonds. Widening is an early warning; tight means calm. Marked n/a this week.
- **Treasury yields / 2s10s:** the interest rate the government pays to borrow for 10 years vs 2 years. The 2s10s spread is the 10-year minus the 2-year; positive and steepening (31bp) is the normal, healthy shape.
- **Breadth:** how many stocks are participating. Broad breadth — most stocks above their 200-day, new highs beating new lows — signals a healthy rally rather than a few giants carrying a tired tape.

*Weekly market letter — September 18, 2026. Built on our daily ranking of ~1,900 US stocks by relative strength.*

More stocks climbed back above their long-term trend, while the Fed narrative turned harder and the short end of the bond market moved to match. Broadening participation on one hand, a less friendly rate backdrop on the other.

## 1. The Fed got the headlines, and they weren't friendly

The loudest macro thread by a wide margin was rates. Coverage this week leaned hawkish, with talk of the Fed narrowing the rate gap, pressure on other central banks to keep up, and US mortgage rates pushing back toward 7%. The bond market agreed with the tone. The 2-year Treasury yield jumped 11 basis points on the week to 4.67%, while the 10-year sat still at 4.94% (down a single basis point). The 2s10s spread is positive at 27bp, but notice which end did the moving: the short maturity, the one that tracks Fed policy most closely. That's the market pricing a central bank that plans to stay higher for longer, not one about to cut.

Why it matters for your money: when short rates grind up and stay up, the most expensive, longest-duration stocks feel it first, the ones whose value lives in profits many years out. Growth and chips carry that duration. Mortgage rates near 7% also press directly on anything housing-touched, worth holding in mind with KB Home and MillerKnoll on next week's calendar.

Inflation is part of why the Fed can't relax: CPI is still running 3.4% year over year, above where the Fed wants it. Jobs look fine but not hot, with unemployment at 4.1% and the last payrolls print at +162k. A labor market cooling gently, not cracking, which gives the Fed room to keep leaning on inflation rather than rushing to protect employment.

## 2. Oil fell, but Iran is rattling the door again

Brent crude dropped 5.1% on the week to 99.29, a real move lower even though the price still starts with a nine. Normally that's a clean tailwind: cheaper energy takes pressure off consumers and off the inflation number the Fed is fighting.

The wrinkle showed up right after the US close. Headlines carried Iran saying the Strait of Hormuz is closed again, the chokepoint roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil passes through. That's a supply-shock risk this week's tape hasn't priced in yet. So you've got a market that spent the week selling oil down, staring at a fresh reason it could snap back. Foreign money already flinched at the combination, with reports of investors pulling cash out of Indian equities, blaming crude and global uncertainty.

For US stocks the read is two-sided. Oil at these levels, and falling, has been a quiet help to the inflation story. But if Hormuz turns into more than a headline, energy is the one place a spike would land hardest and fastest, and it's why the Algo's energy-heavy book (more on that below) isn't as random as it looks.

## 3. The dollar bid, and what Asia is telling us about chips

The US Dollar Index rose 1.1% to 100.2, and USD/JPY climbed 1.1% to 156.9 (with a bit more yen weakness after our close). A firmer dollar is a headwind for US multinationals, since it makes their overseas earnings worth less when translated home, and for commodities priced in dollars. It fits the hawkish-Fed picture: higher-for-longer US rates pull money toward the dollar.

Asia gave a constructive read on chips. Taiwan's TAIEX rose 2.2% on the week and added a touch more after our close, and that index is the cleanest tell on TSMC and the AI semiconductor chain that feeds US names like Nvidia and Broadcom. Korea's KOSPI was flat (-0.2%), a neutral signal on the memory cycle behind Micron. Nikkei up 1.6% with no yen-driven risk scare means the carry trade isn't unwinding, so global risk appetite is intact rather than fragile. Copper, "Dr. Copper," rose 3.4% to 6.69, which argues the global-growth story hasn't rolled over even with China's Hang Seng flat on the week.

Bitcoin, the 24/7 sentiment gauge, rose 4.8% and kept drifting up after equities closed. Add it to the pile: nothing in the risk complex is flashing fear.

## 5. Theme of the week — the GLP-1 machine, end to end

This week's teaching spotlight is the weight-loss drug boom. It's a curated look at one value chain, not a coverage claim on the whole market, and separate from the rotation read above. What makes GLP-1 a great chain to walk is that a single blockbuster drug drags an entire economy behind it, link by link.

Start at the source. The pipeline is the science lab: small biotechs (ALT, GPCR, TERN, VKTX) racing to invent the next, better molecule. High risk, binary outcomes, the lottery-ticket end of the chain. Next come the drug makers, the giants who actually own the winning molecules and print the money: Lilly and Novo Nordisk, with Amgen and Pfizer chasing. This is where the profit pools sit today.

A molecule is useless until someone can make it at scale. That's manufacturing, the picks-and-shovels layer (Charles River, Danaher, Thermo Fisher) that supplies the tools and testing to produce the drug reliably. Then devices, the pens and auto-injectors that get it into a patient (Becton Dickinson, West Pharmaceutical and peers). Then diagnostics, the monitoring gear (Abbott, DexCom, Insulet, Tandem) that tracks the metabolic patients this whole thing serves.

The last two links are where it gets interesting for a regular investor. Telehealth is the delivery counter (Hims & Hers, GoodRx, LifeMD, Teladoc), the platforms that put the drug in front of a customer online. And the "Ozempic effect" is the ripple: what happens to the rest of the economy when tens of millions of people eat less. Snack and soda makers (Hershey, Mondelez, PepsiCo) sit on the wrong side of that, and dialysis (DaVita) on a complicated one.

The way to think about it: money flows to whichever link is the bottleneck, and cost flows away from it. Right now the drug makers are the chokepoint, since they own the molecules everyone wants, which is why the profits concentrate there. The link the market keeps arguing about is telehealth, because that's where the moat is thinnest and the growth is fastest. This week's standout on the numbers is Hims & Hers (HIMS): a price-to-sales ratio of 2.5 against 38% year-over-year revenue growth. The cheapest name in the chain relative to how fast it's growing, a screen, not a recommendation. Do your own homework on why it's cheap; fast growth at a low multiple usually means the market is pricing in a risk you need to understand before you touch it.

Watch it as a live experiment in whether trailing rules protect you when a group turns.

*Methodology. The Algo is a long-only, rules-based paper strategy on a $50,000 notional sleeve, trading the same ~1,900-stock universe. A name is bought only if it clears every gate: its sector ranks near the top of the board with broad participation, earnings are growing year-over-year in both revenue and profit, it isn't already held, and it's past a 21-day cooldown. Qualifying setups are sorted into four conviction tiers by how many independent signals fire — from T1 (multiple 'strong' chart patterns, risked at 1.5% of the sleeve) down to T4 (relative-strength acceleration, 0.5%). Size is set by risk, not dollars: each trade is sized so a stop 2×ATR below entry costs only that tier's risk budget, capped at $2,000–$6,000. Exits are mechanical — scale out a third at +2R and a third at +4R (1R = the initial stop distance), trail the remainder by 2×ATR, book some after a +25% spike, and time out positions left flat after 25 trading days. New entries pause if the book is down 2% on the day or 8% from its peak. Signals are generated after the close and filled the next session at the live price plus 0.15% slippage. These are educational paper results — not advice, and not a live account.*

## 11. Glossary

- **Our rank / the board:** we rank every US stock we track by relative strength, #1 strongest. "#3 in our system" = 3rd-strongest of ~1,900.
- **Relative strength (RS):** performance versus the rest of the market, not in isolation. Rising RS = outperforming.
- **Moving averages (20/50/200-day):** the trend lines. A healthy uptrend has price above a rising 20-day, itself above the 200-day. "Extended" = stretched far above the 20-day and prone to a pullback.
- **Two clocks:** rank is one clock, price is another. When rank rises but price falls, money is often using strength to leave.
- **VIX:** the fear gauge. Below 20 = calm.
- **Credit spreads / high-yield (junk) bonds:** the extra yield risky companies pay to borrow. Widening spreads or falling junk-bond prices are early stress signals; steady ones mean calm.
- **Treasury yields / 2s10s:** the government's borrowing cost for 10 years versus 2 years. A rising 2-year tracks Fed expectations; a rising 10-year pressures expensive, long-dated stocks.
- **Breadth:** how many stocks participate. Two-thirds above their 200-day is broad and healthy.
- **Safe havens (gold, the dollar):** where money runs when it's scared. Flat through a scary headline means the market is calling it noise.

# The Fed Blinked, the Megacaps Carried, and the Board Quietly Reshuffled

*Weekly Market Newsletter · Issue for the week ending 2026-07-31*

The S&P closed the week at 7,489.72, up about 1% and sitting only 1.6% under its June 2 record. That looks calm for a tape where the Fed just held rates through its loudest internal fight in a decade and Apple gave back 7% on an earnings call. Look under the index and it's messier: 1,087 stocks fell this week against 846 that rose. The average stock had a down week. A handful of trillion-dollar names did the lifting, and while they lifted, the board's actual leadership changed hands, out of chips and utilities, into IT consulting, refiners and financials. The gap between the index and the roster of ~1,900 names we rank every day is the story this week.

---

## 1. The lead story: the Fed held, but it was a knife fight

The Fed left its policy rate steady (fed-funds target upper sits at 3.63% on our sheet) despite three officials pushing for an outright hike, the biggest split on the committee since 2016. Prediction markets and at least one large shop had positioned for a surprise hike going in. It didn't come.

You can see why they argued. CPI is still running 3.5% year over year, well north of target, which is the hawks' whole case. But the labor side is cooling: unemployment is 4.2%, the last payrolls print was a soft +57k, and weekly jobless claims ticked up to 197,000. Layoffs are still historically low, so no crisis, just a little air coming out. Sticky inflation against a softening job market splits a central bank down the middle.

The bond market read it as dovish on balance. The 2-year yield dropped 14bp to 4.23% while the 10-year barely moved at 4.68%, steepening the 2s10s spread to a positive 45bp. A steeper curve with the front end falling is the market saying cuts are still the next big move, not hikes, whatever the three hawks want. That's oxygen for banks, which borrow short and lend long, and part of why financials keep climbing the board. The caveat: a 10-year still up near 4.7% keeps a boot on the most expensive, longest-duration stocks, and you can see that in where the chips are ranked.

## 2. Apple ran into a wall it helped build

Apple reported and lost 7.2% on the week, sliding from rank #145 down to #275, a 154-spot drop. The trigger wasn't demand. Tim Cook warned that a memory shortage is about to bite Apple's costs. Memory chips got scarce and expensive, and Apple buys a mountain of them.

That same shortage is a gift to whoever *makes* the memory, which is where the overseas tape gets interesting (more in section 4). The point for now: the thing hurting Apple's margin is the thing that eventually helps Micron and the Korean memory makers. One company's cost is another's revenue.

The rest of megacap earnings cut the other way. Amazon jumped 17% after AWS margins came in strong enough to backstop a $220 billion AI spending plan. Microsoft ripped 21.8%. AbbVie slipped 3.2% as it trimmed its full-year profit outlook to absorb its Apogee acquisition, though it also won EU approval for RINVOQ in two new indications. The tape rewarded cloud and cash flow, and it punished anyone talking about rising input costs.

## 3. Oil fell, and the refiners feasted

Brent finished the week at 90.12, down 6.9%, and the path matters more than the endpoint: crude spiked nearly 4% mid-week on Middle-East and OPEC+ headlines, then gave it all back and more as US–Iran talks and supply chatter took over. Net, oil got cheaper.

Cheaper crude is a feedstock discount for refiners, and our board lit up with them. Par Pacific sits at #2 (+11.2% on the week), World Kinect at #3, HF Sinclair at #8, PBF Energy at #11 (+17.3%), and Calumet at #15. Refiners buy crude and sell gasoline and diesel; when the input drops faster than the product, the spread they earn, the "crack," widens, and the stocks move. This is the *opposite* trade from the integrated majors: ExxonMobil got cut to Neutral at BofA this week. Owning a refiner and owning an oil producer are two different bets on the same barrel, and the board is on the refiner side right now.

Falling oil showed up nowhere else as stress. It's a supply-and-talks story, not a demand scare. That's the read I'd hold onto when you see a scary energy headline: check whether fear spread anywhere else. This week it didn't.

## 5. Theme of the week: reshoring, and why the money's in the robots, not the bricks

Every few years a policy push shows up that's too big to trade as one stock, and rebuilding US factory capacity is this one. Think of it as a chain, the way you'd think about building a restaurant from an empty lot.

First you need the raw stuff, the concrete and steel (Martin Marietta, Nucor, Steel Dynamics, Vulcan). Then you need the firms that pour the foundation and put up the building (Jacobs, Fluor, AECOM, Quanta, MasTec). Then the building has to be outfitted: the electrical backbone that powers it (Eaton, GE Vernova, Hubbell) and, more and more, the automation and scanning gear that runs the line (Emerson, Parker Hannifin, Rockwell, Zebra). Finally the output has to move: rail and warehouses (Union Pacific, CSX, Prologis, Old Dominion).

Here's where the money sits right now. On our node-momentum scores (a 0–100 blend of rank, trend and price-vs-the-50-day, where 50 is the middle of the pack), Automation is the hottest link at 68, with Logistics right behind at 66. The raw materials link is middling at 47, the construction firms lag at 39, and, the surprise, the electrical-gear link is cold at 24. Theme momentum overall is flat, 44 now versus 46 a week ago.

Read that chain and the market's telling you something specific: it's paying up for the equipment that outfits and moves goods through a plant, not the concrete-and-rebar early stages or the power gear. Zebra Technologies is the standout, momentum 86 and ranked #162 of ~1,900, the scanners and sensors that make an automated warehouse work. If you want the value angle instead of the momentum angle, Jacobs screens cheapest on growth-adjusted sales in the group (price-to-sales of 1.2 against 27% revenue growth). That's a screen, not a recommendation, and it's the *lagging* link, so you'd be leaning against the tape.

This is a curated lesson on one value chain, not a claim that reshoring is the whole market or a call to rotate into it. It's how a supply chain concentrates money at its busy points.

## 9. The Algo (educational paper track record)

The paper strategy sits at $50,418, up 0.8% since its late-May inception. Be clear-eyed about the record: 44 closed trades, 11 winners, a 25% win rate, averaging -1.2% per trade. It wins by letting the few winners run far past the many small losers. The two it closed green this week, BNY (+11.6%, +2.87R) and CVS (+9.1%, +1.84R), each earned multiples of what a loser cost. The open book is now heavily financials (JPM, BAC, MET, USB, CFG, MTB, plus fresh adds MA, WEX and WTW) with a health-care tail (DVA +13%, TMO, IQV), the same rotation the rankings are showing, expressed as trades. New entries this week (MA, WTW, DRH, RHP) are barely off their entry price, so the jury's out.

*Methodology. The Algo is a long-only, rules-based paper strategy on a $50,000 notional sleeve, trading the same ~1,900-stock universe. A name is bought only if it clears every gate: its sector ranks near the top of the board with broad participation, earnings are growing year-over-year in both revenue and profit, it isn't already held, and it's past a 21-day cooldown. Qualifying setups are sorted into four conviction tiers by how many independent signals fire — from T1 (multiple 'strong' chart patterns, risked at 1.5% of the sleeve) down to T4 (relative-strength acceleration, 0.5%). Size is set by risk, not dollars: each trade is sized so a stop 2×ATR below entry costs only that tier's risk budget, capped at $2,000–$6,000. Exits are mechanical — scale out a third at +2R and a third at +4R (1R = the initial stop distance), trail the remainder by 2×ATR, book some after a +25% spike, and time out positions left flat after 25 trading days. New entries pause if the book is down 2% on the day or 8% from its peak. Signals are generated after the close and filled the next session at the live price plus 0.15% slippage. These are educational paper results — not advice, and not a live account.*

## 11. Glossary

- **Our rank / the board:** we sort every US stock we track by relative strength, #1 strongest. "#42 in our system" = 42nd-strongest of ~1,900.
- **Relative strength (RS):** performance versus the rest of the market, not in isolation. Rising RS = outperforming.
- **Moving averages (20/50/200-day):** the trend lines. A healthy uptrend has price above a rising 20-day, itself above the 200-day. "Extended" means far above the 20-day and prone to a pullback.
- **Crack spread:** the margin a refiner earns between the crude it buys and the fuel it sells. Cheaper crude with steady fuel prices = a wider crack.
- **2s10s spread:** the 10-year Treasury yield minus the 2-year. Positive and steepening (45bp now) usually means the market expects rate cuts ahead.
- **VIX:** the fear gauge. Below 20 is calm.
- **Credit spreads / high-yield (junk) bonds:** the extra yield risky companies pay over Treasuries. Widening spreads or falling junk-bond prices are early stress signals; steady is calm.
- **Safe havens (gold, the dollar):** where money hides when scared. Flat gold and a soft dollar through a scary headline means the market is treating it as noise.
- **Node momentum:** a 0–100 score blending RS rank, trend and price-vs-the-50-day; 50 is the middle of the universe.
